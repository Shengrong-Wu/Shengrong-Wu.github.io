Local Well-Posedness for Quasilinear Wave Equations Near Intersecting Null Hypersurfaces

1. Introduction

The model equation in this note is [ g^{}(u,u),u = F(u,u), ] where (u) is a scalar unknown, (g^{}) is a symmetric Lorentzian coefficient matrix depending on (u) and its first derivatives, and (F) is a lower-order nonlinearity.

This is a quasilinear wave equation. It belongs to one of the central classes of nonlinear hyperbolic PDE. The basic local question is:

Given suitable initial data, does there exist a unique solution for short time, and does the solution depend continuously on the data?

For standard Cauchy data on a spacelike hypersurface, the answer is classical: one reduces the equation to a first-order symmetric hyperbolic system and applies energy estimates. This theory was built in the modern PDE framework by Kato, Hughes–Kato–Marsden, Hörmander, Taylor, and many others.

A second, more geometric problem is the characteristic initial value problem: instead of prescribing data on a spacelike slice, one prescribes data on one or two null hypersurfaces. This problem is fundamental in general relativity, geometric optics, and shock formation. For the double-null problem, the foundational local existence theorem is due to Rendall, who reduced the characteristic problem to a spacelike Cauchy problem. In general relativity, Luk later extended the existence region beyond the tiny corner neighborhood produced by Rendall’s method.

Broadly speaking, modern wave equation theory has several major directions:

  • Local well-posedness for quasilinear hyperbolic equations, via symmetric hyperbolic reduction and energy estimates.
  • Dispersive and Strichartz estimates, used for low-regularity theory and semilinear/critical equations.
  • Null structure and vector field methods, central in small-data global existence and Einstein equations.
  • Shock formation, where one proves the finite-time breakdown of classical solutions for quasilinear waves.
  • Geometric wave equations, such as wave maps, Yang–Mills, Maxwell–Klein–Gordon, and Einstein equations.
  • Wave equations on black hole backgrounds, including decay, scattering, and nonlinear stability questions.

Some representative modern developments include:

  • large-data/global regularity results for geometric wave equations such as wave maps;
  • sharp estimates for waves on rough backgrounds and black hole spacetimes;
  • nonlinear stability theory for the Einstein equations;
  • stable shock formation for quasilinear wave equations;
  • renewed work on characteristic and null-boundary formulations for Einstein-type systems.

The goal of this note is narrower: I will explain a clean local theorem near the corner [ S = N_1 N_2 ] for the characteristic problem on two intersecting null hypersurfaces (N_1) and (N_2), following the classical strategy of Rendall and later geometric PDE expositions.

2. The theorem and the proof strategy

2.1. Geometric setup

Let [ N_1 = {u=0, v}, N_2 = {v=0, u}, ] be two smooth null hypersurfaces intersecting transversely at [ S = {u=v=0}. ] We use local coordinates [ (u,v,^A),A=2,,n, ] near (S). The unknown is a scalar function (z), and we study [ g^{}(z,z),z = F(z,z). ]

We assume:

  1. (g^{}(z,p)) and (F(z,p)) are smooth in ((z,p)).
  2. (g{}=g{}).
  3. In a neighborhood of the range of the data, (g^{}) is Lorentzian.
  4. (N_1) and (N_2) are characteristic, i.e. [ g^{uu}=0 N_1, g^{vv}=0 N_2. ]

2.2. What data should be prescribed?

For a generic equation with principal coefficients depending on (z), the safest rigorous formulation is not “prescribe arbitrary traces of (z)”. Instead one prescribes a reduced characteristic data set that determines a full formal jet on (N_1N_2).

A convenient reduced data set is: [ z|_{N_1}=1, z|{N_2}=_2, u z|{N_1}=_1, v z|{N_2}=_2, ] subject to:

  • the corner compatibility conditions [ _1|_S = _2|_S, _A_1|_S = _A_2|_S, _v_1|_S = _2|_S, _1|_S = _u_2|_S, ]
  • the characteristic constraints [ g^{uu}(_1,_1,_v_1,_A_1)=0 N_1, ] [ g^{vv}(_2,_u_2,_2,_A_2)=0 N_2, ]
  • and all higher-order compatibility relations obtained by formally differentiating the equation.

This is the correct smooth data class in Rendall-style characteristic existence theorems.

2.3. Main theorem

We now state the local corner theorem.

Theorem (local existence near (N_1N_2)).
Consider [ g^{}(z,z),z = F(z,z) ] in a neighborhood of (S=N_1N_2), where (g,F) are smooth and (g) is Lorentzian.
Assume a smooth reduced characteristic data set on (N_1N_2) is given, satisfying the null constraints and all compatibility conditions, and assume these data determine a smooth formal jet on (N_1N_2).
Then there exists (>0) and a unique smooth solution [ z C^(), ={0u, 0v}, ] which realizes the prescribed characteristic data.

2.4. What this article will do

The proof has four main steps:

  1. Rewrite the scalar second-order equation as a first-order symmetric hyperbolic system.
  2. Derive the basic (H^s) energy estimate for the corresponding spacelike Cauchy problem.
  3. Explain how the characteristic data determine all formal derivatives along (N_1N_2).
  4. Invoke Rendall’s reduction of the characteristic problem to a standard Cauchy problem, then recover the scalar solution and prove uniqueness.

2.5. Main references

The proof strategy is based primarily on:

  • A. D. Rendall, Reduction of the characteristic initial value problem to the Cauchy problem and its applications to the Einstein equations, Proc. Roy. Soc. Lond. A 427 (1990), 221–239.
  • J. Luk, On the Local Existence for the Characteristic Initial Value Problem in General Relativity, IMRN 2012.
  • Standard local theory for symmetric hyperbolic systems: Kato, Hughes–Kato–Marsden, Hörmander, Taylor.
  • For direct characteristic estimates at finite regularity: Dossa, Tadmon, Müller zum Hagen, etc.

3. Estimates

In this section we derive the energy estimate needed for the spacelike Cauchy problem.

3.1. First-order reduction

Introduce a spacelike time function [ t = u+v, ] and spatial variables [ x=(x1,,xn). ] Rewrite the equation as [ a^{}(z,z),z = F(z,z), ] where (,,1,,n), (x^0=t), and (a^{}) is symmetric.

Define [ q := _t z, w_i := _i z, V := (q,w_1,,w_n). ] Then [ _t z = q, ] and the second-order equation becomes [ a^{00}(z,q,w),_t q + 2a^{0j}(z,q,w),_j q + a^{ij}(z,q,w),_i w_j = F(z,q,w). ] Moreover, [ _t w_i - _i q = 0. ]

So ((z,V)) satisfies a first-order quasilinear system [ A^0(z,V),_t V + A^j(z,V),_j V = G(z,V), ] where the coefficient matrices can be chosen as [ A^0= \[\begin{pmatrix} -a^{00} & 0\\[1mm] 0 & (a^{ij}) \end{pmatrix}\] , A^k= \[\begin{pmatrix} -2a^{0k} & -(a^{k1},\dots,a^{kn})\\[1mm] -(a^{1k},\dots,a^{nk})^T & 0 \end{pmatrix}\]

, ] and [ G(z,V)=(-F(z,V),0,,0)^T. ]

If the slices (t=) are spacelike, then [ -a^{00} >0, a^{ij}_i_j ||^2, ] so (A^0) is positive definite. Since (a{ij}=a{ji}), the matrices (A^k) are symmetric. Thus (3.5) is a symmetric hyperbolic system.

3.2. Energy for the linearized problem

For (sN), define [ E_s(t) := {||s} {_t} A0,U,^U,dx, ] where (_t={x^0=t}).

This is the key a priori estimate.

3.4. Nonlinear estimate

For the nonlinear system (3.5), the matrices (A^(z,V)) and source term (G(z,V)) depend smoothly on ((z,V)). Therefore, again by Moser estimates, [ |A(z,V)|_{Hs}+|G(z,V)|{H^s} P(|z|{Hs}+|V|_{Hs}), ] for some polynomial (P).

Thus the nonlinear energy satisfies [ E_s(t) P(E_s(t)). ] This gives short-time control of the (H^s)-norm, provided (s) is large enough. For a principal part depending on (z), one usually takes [ s>n2+2, ] so that the coefficients are at least (C^1) by Sobolev embedding.

4. Proof of the theorem

We now prove the local characteristic existence theorem.

4.1. Step 1: characteristic transport identities

Write [ p_u=_u z, p_v=_v z, p_A=_A z. ] Then the equation becomes [ g^{uu}_u p_u + 2g^{uv}_u p_v + 2g^{uA}_A p_u + g^{vv}_v p_v + 2g^{vA}_A p_v + g^{AB}_A p_B = F(z,p). ]

On (N_1={u=0}), since (g^{uu}=0), [ 2g^{uv},_u p_v = F - g^{vv}_v p_v - 2g^{vA}_A p_v - g^{AB}_A p_B - 2g^{uA}A p_u. ] All terms on the right are known from the reduced data on (N_1), namely [ z|{N_1}=1, p_u|{N_1}=1, p_v|{N_1}=_v1, p_A|{N_1}=_A_1. ] Therefore (u p_v|{N_1}) is determined.

Similarly, on (N_2={v=0}), since (g^{vv}=0), [ 2g^{uv},_v p_u = F - g^{uu}_u p_u - 2g^{uA}_A p_u - g^{AB}_A p_B - 2g^{vA}_A p_v. ] Hence (v p_u|{N_2}) is determined.

By repeatedly differentiating (4.2) and (4.4) tangentially and commuting derivatives, one recursively determines all higher formal derivatives along (N_1N_2), provided the compatibility conditions hold.

This is exactly why the theorem is stated in terms of a reduced characteristic data set that determines a smooth formal jet.

4.2. Step 2: reduction to a symmetric hyperbolic system

By Section 3, the scalar equation is equivalent to the symmetric hyperbolic system [ A^0(z,V)_t V + A^j(z,V)_j V = G(z,V), ] together with [ _t z = q. ]

Thus the original problem is reduced to a first-order quasilinear symmetric hyperbolic problem.

4.3. Step 3: spacelike auxiliary problem

Rendall’s key idea is to replace the characteristic problem by an auxiliary Cauchy problem on spacelike hypersurfaces approaching (N_1N_2).

Very schematically: - use the formal jet on (N_1N_2), - extend it smoothly to a spacelike hypersurface (_0) through the corner (S), - solve the standard spacelike Cauchy problem, - then show the resulting solution has the prescribed characteristic traces.

This step uses Whitney-type extension of the formal jet and the standard (H^s) local existence theorem for symmetric hyperbolic systems.

Because the symmetric hyperbolic Cauchy problem is locally well posed by the estimate (3.15), one obtains a smooth solution [ (z,V)C^(_) ] for some (>0).

4.4. Step 4: recover the scalar wave equation

We must check that the first-order unknowns really come from derivatives of (z). Define [ C_i := w_i - _i z. ] Using [ _t z=q, _t w_i-_i q=0, ] we compute [ _t C_i = _t w_i - _i_t z = _i q - _i q = 0. ] If the spacelike initial data on (0) are chosen consistently, then [ C_i|{0}=0. ] Therefore [ C_i . ] So (w_i=i z), (q=t z), and substituting back into the first-order system yields the original second-order equation [ g^{}(z,z),z = F(z,z). ]

4.5. Step 5: uniqueness

Suppose (z,z) are two smooth solutions with the same characteristic data. Their first-order variables satisfy the same symmetric hyperbolic system. The difference (W) then solves a linear symmetric hyperbolic equation of the form [ A^0 _t W + A^j _j W = C W, ] with zero data. The energy estimate implies [ W. ] Hence [ z=z. ]

This proves existence and uniqueness.

5. Open problems and the relation with Einstein equations

5.1. Open problems in wave equations

Even though local well-posedness is classical in smooth settings, many major questions remain open.

(a) Low-regularity characteristic theory

For spacelike Cauchy data, low-regularity well-posedness is highly developed. For characteristic data, especially for fully quasilinear systems, the optimal regularity threshold is still much less understood.

(b) Direct null energy methods for quasilinear systems

Rendall’s theorem reduces the null problem to a spacelike one. A more intrinsic theory would prove well-posedness directly in null geometry with optimal estimates and minimal derivative loss.

(c) Sharp continuation criteria

For quasilinear waves, one wants precise geometric criteria telling exactly when a smooth solution can be continued. In many problems the breakdown mechanism is either: - blow-up of (u), - loss of hyperbolicity, - or shock formation.

A sharp theorem distinguishing these mechanisms remains difficult in general settings.

(d) Shock formation beyond small-data models

Shock formation is now understood in several important quasilinear settings, but there is still much to do for systems, nontrivial backgrounds, and large-data regimes.

(e) Quasilinear wave equations on curved backgrounds

One wants local and global theories adapted to rough Lorentzian metrics, black hole backgrounds, or spacetimes generated by Einstein equations themselves.

(f) Characteristic boundary value problems

Null-timelike and null-initial-boundary value problems are important in numerical relativity and geometric analysis, but the general well-posedness theory is subtler than the pure double-null problem.

5.2. Why this theorem matters for Einstein equations

Einstein’s equations in a suitable gauge reduce schematically to a system of quasilinear wave equations: [ g^{}(h,h),h_{} = N_{}(h,h). ] Thus the theorem proved above is the local model for the double-null characteristic initial value problem in general relativity.

This is important because:

  1. Gravitational radiation is naturally described along null hypersurfaces.
  2. Many geometric constructions in relativity use double-null coordinates.
  3. Characteristic data are central in black hole formation, impulsive waves, and scattering theory.
  4. Large parts of modern Einstein analysis combine:
    • local quasilinear wave theory,
    • null transport equations,
    • geometric energy estimates,
    • and delicate structure such as null forms.

In the Einstein setting, the free data are not arbitrary scalar traces: they consist of geometric quantities such as induced conformal geometry and null second fundamental forms, subject to null constraint equations. Still, the local existence philosophy is exactly the same: [ . ]

5.3. A practical lesson for beginners

If you are learning Einstein equations or geometric wave equations, this theorem is worth mastering because it teaches three universal ideas:

  • reduce a second-order geometric equation to a first-order hyperbolic system;
  • derive robust energy estimates in Sobolev spaces;
  • understand how geometric initial data determine analytic compatibility conditions.

Those three themes appear everywhere in nonlinear wave analysis.

Closing remark

The theorem proved here is local and corner-based. It is the natural starting point for more ambitious results: - extending the solution farther along null hypersurfaces, - proving global existence under smallness assumptions, - describing singularity formation, - and applying the entire framework to Einstein’s equations.

For a first blog post on quasilinear wave equations, this is the right level of theorem: geometric, rigorous, and already rich enough to connect directly with modern relativity.
